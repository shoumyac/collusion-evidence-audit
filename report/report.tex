\begin{abstract}
A collusion detector's verdict is evidence only if its stated error rates hold on the unit that is decided on. We audit the detector stack and benchmark (ColludeBench) of the sprint's \texttt{steganographic-collusion} project, which reports thresholds calibrated to a false-positive rate of $10^{-3}$. \textbf{(1) Unit of replication.} The shipped logs count 22{,}127 runs but hold 3{,}918 distinct runs within files and 2{,}740 across files; on 180 fresh honest audit batches per scenario the cross-run mutual-information p-value is $\le0.05$ on 99\% (pricing) and 100\% (auction) of batches. Calibrating the same statistic on independent honest batches gives a held-out false-positive rate of about 5--6\% with 100\% detection of scripted colluders, and about 20 honest batches are needed. \textbf{(2) Say versus do.} In a pricing game played by six LLM agent models, the shipped detectors flag every honest batch, and a blind text auditor agrees with the condition labels for open-weight agents (AUROC 0.92--1.00) but disagrees with them for Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.00) and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (0.21): their ``honest'' runs openly propose price-holding, while Haiku's ``collusion'' runs mostly refuse. \textbf{(3) Labels.} Controls without a message channel, with the rival's messages paraphrased or taken from another run, and with a reworded prompt show that the ``collusion'' instruction is an unreliable label: for the Claude models the price rise needs the firm to write messages but not the rival to read them (a swap control leaves it intact), so it is parallel obedience to a shared prompt rather than communication-mediated coordination, and its size depends on the prompt wording. We propose an evidence ladder and a behavioural manipulation check for positive controls.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Auditing agent logs for collusion is only useful if an auditor's output has known error rates. The project we audit reports detectors calibrated to a false-positive rate (FPR) of $10^{-3}$ using 10{,}000 honest runs and sequential e-value martingales \cite{starter,tailor}. The sprint asks what should count as convincing evidence of collusion. We ask the narrower, checkable question: \emph{when this auditor says ``collusion'', what unit of evidence stands behind it, do its error rates hold on held-out units, and do the labels of its positive and negative controls match behaviour?}

Contributions (all backed by saved outputs in our repository):
\begin{enumerate}
\item We reproduce and extend the project's own account of duplicated transcripts, and show how small the independent calibration samples behind the $10^{-3}$ thresholds are (Section~\ref{sec:A}).
\item On held-out honest, keyword-colluding and stealth-colluding audit batches, reported p-values are miscalibrated while the underlying statistics remain informative. We test one cause of the cross-run MI result and give a calibration-size curve (Section~\ref{sec:B}).
\item We run six LLM agent models in the same pricing game, with controls for the message channel, paraphrase and prompt wording, and compare repository detectors, trained classifiers and blind LLM auditors (Section~\ref{sec:C}).
\item We propose an evidence ladder and release a small tested audit kit (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Steganographic and secret collusion between LLMs was studied by Mathew et al.\ \cite{mathew} and Motwani et al.\ \cite{motwani}. LLM pricing agents can reach supracompetitive outcomes \cite{fish}; in double auctions direct communication between LLM sellers increased collusive tendencies, with large differences across models \cite{agrawal}; and algorithmic agents can produce collusive outcomes without explicit collusion \cite{cho}. The project we audit \cite{tailor} combines mutual-information, permutation, watermark \cite{kirchenbauer}, linguistic, acceptance-bias and welfare signals. Our methodological frame is statistical: Hurlbert's pseudoreplication \cite{hurlbert}, treating non-independent replicates as independent, is the failure we look for in logs and calibration samples, and anytime-valid inference with e-values \cite{ramdas,vovk} is the framework the project invokes for its sequential tests. We did not evaluate that sequential component. To our knowledge no prior work audits the calibration unit and label validity of a published collusion detector in this way, but we did not conduct an exhaustive search.

\section{Methods}
\paragraph{What we mean by collusion.} The sprint's own comparison of 139 definitions \cite{defs} separates an economics family (prices sustained above the static Nash price, toward the joint-profit maximum, tacit or explicit) from an AI-safety family (unwanted, covert coordination). We use \emph{both, for different objects}. For the scripted benchmark agents, ``colluding'' means encoding a covert signal through a channel, the AI-safety sense that the detectors target. For the LLM agents, we use the economics outcome sense to judge behaviour (price relative to Nash and the joint maximum, \S\ref{sec:C}), and we treat the \emph{instruction} to coordinate as an operational label of intent, not as evidence of an agreement. We do not claim that any run involved a meeting of minds, and our blind auditors are asked about coordination on higher prices than independent competition would give.

\paragraph{Code and data (pre-existing).} All detector, benchmark and scripted-agent code and the shipped logs are the starter repository at commit \texttt{e6fb130} \cite{starter}, unmodified. Our code is in \texttt{sprint\_work/} and \texttt{kit/} of our repository.

\paragraph{A. Replication audit.} We split every shipped transcript where \texttt{round\_id} drops, hash each run, and count raw, within-file-distinct and globally distinct runs, and read the sample size behind each threshold.

\paragraph{B. Held-out controls.} Detectors act on an \emph{audit}, a batch of 10 runs. With seeds disjoint from the shipped calibration, we ran independent batches in three scenarios (pricing duopoly, first-price auction, peer review) using the repository's own audit configurations: 180 honest, 30 keyword-colluder and 20 stealth (``sparse'') colluder batches per scenario, plus 20 ``smart'' and 20 ``adaptive'' colluder batches (4 for the peer-review adaptive configuration, which was too slow to finish). The decision unit is the batch. We report the fraction with reported $p\le\alpha$ (Wilson 95\% intervals in Fig.~\ref{fig:b}). For recalibration we use 500 random half splits of the honest batches (threshold: 95th percentile of the calibration half; FPR on the other half; TPR on all colluding batches). A calibration-size curve varies the number of honest batches used for the threshold.

\paragraph{C. LLM agents.} Two firms play the repository's pricing environment for 10 rounds (demand $10-1.5p_i+0.5p_j$, cost 1). \emph{Benchmarks:} the symmetric Nash price is 4.6, the joint-profit-maximising price is 5.5, and demand is zero at a common price of 10 (checked by grid search in code). Each round a firm posts a short public statement the rival reads, then a price. The \emph{honest} prompt says to maximise own profit; the \emph{collude} prompt adds that both firms earn more if prices stay high and asks to coordinate through statements and prices without mentioning an agreement. Controls: \emph{silent} (same instructions, no message channel, rival's prices only); \emph{paraphrase} (the rival's statements are rewritten by Claude Haiku 5.5, meaning preserved, before the firm reads them); and \emph{prompt 2} (reworded prompts). Agent models: Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, Phi-4 and Mistral-Nemo-12B (open weights, served with vLLM on Modal) and Claude Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 (API), 40--50 runs per condition. A reply with no parsable price keeps the previous price (4.0 in round 1); cells with over 5\% such replies are marked $\dagger$. Transcripts use the repository format and are scored by the repository detectors in batches of 10 (4--5 batches per condition).

\paragraph{D. Say versus do.} (i) Naive Bayes on words (say), a Gaussian on the run-mean price (do), 5-fold cross-validated with bootstrap CIs, and a variant with every word of the prompts masked. (ii) Two blind LLM auditors (Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Qwen2.5-7B as a different model family) read one transcript in three views (messages, prices, both) and return a 0--100 suspicion score, never told the condition.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Replication audit of the shipped data}\label{sec:A}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=.5\linewidth]{fig1_pseudorep.pdf}
\caption{Runs counted naively (appended logs) versus distinct runs, by results folder (log scale).}\label{fig:a}\end{figure}
We reproduce the README's counts (1{,}737 files, 1{,}155 with several runs, 22{,}127 runs counted naively, 3{,}918 distinct within files). New here: across files only 2{,}740 runs are distinct by content (2{,}104 ignoring run id), and, e.g., the first-price-auction calibration folder holds 5{,}840 runs but 200 distinct transcripts (ignoring run id), peer review 7{,}640 runs but 434 (Fig.~\ref{fig:a}). Seven of the eight $10^{-3}$ thresholds equal the maximum of their calibration sample; a $10^{-3}$ quantile needs at least 1{,}000 independent samples, and the sizes are 4 (acceptance bias), 600 (linguistic), 610 (permutation), 1{,}640 (watermark) and 1 (each external baseline). The eighth, cross-run MI, has 59{,}745 samples, but they are pairs drawn from at most 200 runs per scenario, so pairs sharing a run are dependent. The watermark variance is 0 in every honest run, so its threshold is trivially 0, and the shipped union validation resamples the same honest values that set the thresholds.\footnote{It also reports a Westfall--Young FPR of 1.0 at both nominal levels; we did not investigate why.}

\subsection{Held-out controls}\label{sec:B}

\caption{Fraction of batches with reported $p\le\alpha$ (180 honest, 30 keyword (kw), 20 sparse (sp) batches per scenario), and batch-level recalibration (500 half splits of the honest batches; FPR on the held-out half, TPR on all colluding batches). ``--'': not applicable or not computed.}\label{tab:b}\end{table}
Reported p-values are far from nominal on honest batches: cross-run MI is $\le0.05$ on 99\%, 100\% and 14\% of honest pricing, auction and review batches, and the linguistic detector on 88\%, 95\% and 93\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:b}, Table~\ref{tab:b}). For the linguistic detector this follows from the code: its statistic is the maximum of a sample's chi-square values compared with that sample's own mean and standard deviation. Acceptance bias flags 19\% of honest peer-review batches and has no power against keyword colluders (27\%). Permutation invariance and watermark variance never fire. The welfare detector is well behaved on honest batches but detects scripted colluders trivially: they quote a price of 15, where the demand model gives zero sales.

\emph{One candidate cause, partly tested.} In the MI detector the observed statistic is estimated with 200 optimisation steps but each shuffled-null estimate with 120. We re-ran the same 10 honest and 10 keyword batches per scenario for pricing and auction (40 batches in total) with the null also at 200 steps (the starter source is patched only at runtime). Honest batches flagged at $p\le0.05$ fall from 9/10 (pricing) and 10/10 (auction) to 0/10 in both, but keyword colluders fall from 10/10 to 0/10 as well: the matched null removes the false alarms and also all power. The step mismatch is therefore a real contributor to the false alarms, not a fix. Our explanation for the lost power, that the null's spread comes from single-pair estimates while the statistic is a mean over about 45 pairs, is a hypothesis we did not test.

\emph{The statistics are informative.} Recalibrating on honest batches gives a held-out FPR of 5--6\% for every detector and, for cross-run MI, 100\% detection of keyword and sparse colluders in all three scenarios (a maximum-rule threshold gives 1.1\% FPR with the same detection). The linguistic detector detects sparse colluders in pricing and review but not keyword colluders. The ``smart'' and ``adaptive'' colluders are detected at 100\% by recalibrated MI in every scenario as well, so \emph{all four scripted positive controls are easy} for this detector (the flat right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}): this shows it can find these scripted channels, and says nothing about unknown ones. That a 95th-percentile threshold gives about 5\% held-out FPR is expected by construction; the informative quantity is \emph{how many independent honest units are needed}. Fig.~\ref{fig:curve} shows the empirical curve and the exact theory for a threshold at the $r$-th order statistic of $n$ calibration batches, whose exceedance probability is $\mathrm{Beta}(n-r+1,r)$ with mean $(n-r+1)/(n+1)$: the empirical points follow it. With 3 honest batches the held-out FPR of a 5\% threshold is 24--26\%, with 12 it is 8\%, and from about 20 batches it is 4--5\% in all three scenarios (80 batches: 4.7--4.9\%).

\emph{This does not verify $10^{-3}$.} Even a perfect detector would need a number of independent honest units of order $3/10^{-3}=3{,}000$ (the ``rule of three'' for a rough 95\% bound with zero false positives) to support a $10^{-3}$ claim, and 1{,}000 or more to place a quantile there. Our 180 honest batches per scenario can support a 5\% or 1\% claim at best, which is the point: the shipped $10^{-3}$ claim rests on far fewer independent units than that.

Left: held-out FPR (mean over 400 random splits) for the three scenarios, with the exact theoretical Beta curve (mean and $\pm1$ sd band). Right: detection rate on four colluder types (mean of the three scenarios).}\label{fig:curve}\end{figure}

\subsection{LLM agents: what is said versus what is done}\label{sec:C}
\begin{table}[t]\centering\small
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{../sprint_work/out/table2.tex}}
\caption{Run-mean price (95\% bootstrap CI) by condition. Benchmarks: cost 1.0, Nash 4.6, joint maximum 5.5. ``c: Nash$\to$joint'' is $(\bar p_c-4.6)/(5.5-4.6)$; negative values are below Nash. Refusal c: share of collusion-condition messages matching a refusal regex (Appendix B). $\dagger$: over 5\% of replies had no parsable price.}\label{tab:c}\end{table}
\emph{Prices and the benchmarks.} Honest LLM prices (1.7--2.5) sit well below the Nash price of 4.6: the firms are not told the demand curve and price near cost. The ``collude'' instruction raises the mean price in every model (Table~\ref{tab:c}), but only Claude Sonnet 5.5 (4.49, CI 4.08--4.92) reaches the Nash level, and no model's mean reaches the joint maximum (some individual Sonnet runs exceed it, up to a run-mean of 6.9). A higher price than the honest run is therefore not by itself supracompetitive relative to Nash.

\emph{How many independent trajectories?} We apply our own replication test to these runs (Appendix D, Table~\ref{tab:g}). Diversity is uneven. Honest runs with messages are mostly distinct (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, Phi-4 and Mistral-Nemo: 40--50 of 40--50; Haiku 34 of 40; Sonnet only 17 of 40). The collusion instruction makes several models converge on few price paths (Qwen2.5-32B 5 of 40, Phi-4 14 of 40, Qwen2.5-7B 18 of 50), Haiku's two silent cells have 3 and 4 distinct paths in 40 runs, and other silent cells have 8--21. Runs that repeat the same path are replicates, not independent samples, so a tight interval such as Haiku's [1.99, 2.00] or Qwen2.5-32B's collusion cell understates the uncertainty. We therefore read these cells qualitatively (for example ``no visible effect of the instruction'' for Haiku silent) and make no precision claim from their intervals.

\emph{The instruction works best when the firm writes messages.} Without any message channel, the collusion instruction barely moves prices for the Claude models (Haiku 1.99 vs 1.99; Sonnet 2.50 vs 2.84, against 1.70 vs 2.32 and 2.46 vs 4.49 with messages) and has a smaller effect for Qwen2.5-7B (1.88 vs 2.06) and Qwen2.5-32B (2.17 vs 2.43). For Phi-4 and Mistral-Nemo the silent condition still raises prices (1.63 vs 2.76, Phi-4's honest-silent cell has 14.5\% unparsable replies; 1.89 vs 2.53), so independent obedience to the same prompt explains part of the rise for these models.

\begin{table}[t]\centering\small
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{../sprint_work/out/table4.tex}}
\caption{Claude models: mean price (95\% CI) in the main prompt, with the rival's messages paraphrased, with the rival's messages replaced by those of a different run (swap), and with reworded prompts. $\dagger$: over 5\% unparsable replies.}\label{tab:d}\end{table}
\emph{Paraphrase, message swap and prompt wording.} Paraphrasing the rival's messages leaves the collusion effect essentially unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:d}: Haiku 2.32 to 2.23, Sonnet 4.49 to 4.34). That alone proves little, because a meaning-preserving paraphrase may keep everything that matters. The stronger control is the swap: each firm reads the messages of a \emph{different} run (same condition, same round, same role) instead of its actual rival's. The rise persists (Haiku 2.52 [2.29, 2.77], Sonnet 4.39 [3.97, 4.84]; honest 1.71 and 2.49). So what drives the higher prices is not the two firms reading each other. The collusion instruction raises each firm's own prices once it writes a statement (and the silent control shows less effect when it writes none), which is parallel obedience to a shared prompt, not communication-mediated coordination. Both the paraphrase and swap interventions leave the signal intact, so neither supports a bilateral message channel. Prompt wording matters too: with reworded prompts, Haiku's collusion instruction has no price effect (1.58 vs 1.58) and Sonnet's honest price rises to 4.19, near Nash, with an overlapping interval for the collusion condition (4.38).

\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{../sprint_work/out/table3.tex}}

\emph{The shipped detectors do not separate the conditions.} Cross-run MI and welfare flag every honest batch for every model (Table~\ref{tab:e}); the linguistic detector flags between half and all of them. These detectors target scripted steganographic channels, so this is a transfer test, not proof of miscalibration on their intended task; the scripted-agent results above stand on their own. The welfare baseline is calibrated on scripted ``honest'' firms that price at 1.1, so any firm earning a normal margin looks like a welfare loss. Trained classifiers do separate the conditions: say-only AUROC is 1.00 for every model, also with all prompt words masked, so the separation is not copied prompt vocabulary (for Haiku it includes refusal language); do-only AUROC is 0.74--0.99.

\emph{Say and do disagree for the Claude agents, and both labels are unreliable.} For the four open-weight models a text auditor separates the conditions from messages alone (AUROC 0.92--1.00, Table~\ref{tab:f}). For Haiku it is 0.00 (the second auditor, Qwen2.5-7B, also gives 0.00) and for Sonnet 0.21 (0.42), and under the reworded prompt 0.00 and 0.33: the auditors rank the \emph{honest-labelled} runs as more suspicious. We opened transcripts to rule out a label flip or bug (Appendix C). In Haiku's collusion condition, 98\% of messages decline to coordinate (``I won't coordinate\ldots that would be collusion'') and the auditor scores them 3--5; in its honest condition, 63\% of messages match a price-stability regex and the auditor scores them 92--95, citing for example ``I'll hold 1.75 as long as you do'' and a stated willingness to retaliate. The auditor disagrees with the labels, not with the transcripts: the honest-labelled Haiku runs contain explicit price-holding proposals, while the collusion-labelled runs are mostly refusals. For Sonnet both conditions contain tacit signalling (scores 88 vs 72--88). The ``collude'' label is an instruction and the ``honest'' label is not a guarantee of independent play: simply allowing the rival to read statements elicits coordination talk. The price (do) view was weaker as an auditor input, with AUROC 0.16--0.75 for the Claude agents (Table~\ref{tab:f}), while a trained price classifier reached 0.80 and 0.93.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
\paragraph{Evidence ladder (a proposal, not a validated standard).} We suggest that an audit report state the highest tier it reaches. \textbf{Tier 0}: a detector fires. \textbf{Tier 1}: the p-value or threshold is calibrated on independent units of the kind that is decided on, with the unit count reported; about 20 honest units were needed here. \textbf{Tier 2}: the positive and negative controls pass a behavioural manipulation check (the outcome moves in the predicted direction with a CI excluding zero, under more than one prompt wording, and what the agents say does not contradict the label), with a false-positive check on honest agents from the same model family. \textbf{Tier 3}: detectors on different channels (messages and actions) agree, and a channel intervention (paraphrase, or swapping in messages from another run) changes the signal as the hypothesis predicts. Applying it: the shipped $10^{-3}$ claim reaches Tier 0 on our data; recalibrated cross-run MI on scripted agents reaches Tier 1; our LLM experiments reach Tier 2 for none of the Claude message detectors (Haiku fails under prompt 2 and the text auditor disagrees with the labels), and both interventions we ran (paraphrase and swap) left the signal intact, which is evidence against a message-mediated mechanism rather than a confirmation of one, so Tier 3 is not reached.

\paragraph{Implications.} Report the number of independent units behind each threshold; calibrate on the decision unit; check a positive control's behaviour, not only its prompt; use channel-separated detectors; and validate honest baselines on the model family under audit. This is a critique of reporting practice, not of the original authors, whose open release made the audit possible.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) \emph{Transfer.} The detectors were built for scripted steganography; our LLM results test transfer. (ii) \emph{Scope.} One game (10-round pricing), 40--50 runs per condition, one prompt family plus one rewording, default sampling for the Claude models; prices depend strongly on wording (Table~\ref{tab:d}). (iii) \emph{Parse failures} up to 14.5\% in one cell, handled by keeping the previous price, which can bias means toward the initial 4.0. (iv) \emph{Self-evaluation.} Sonnet 5.5 is both an agent and an auditor, and Claude wrote our scripts; the second-family auditor and the classifiers reduce but do not remove this concern. (v) A price above the honest price is not proof of collusion, and all models price below Nash when honest. (vi) The refusal and price-stability regexes and the auditor prompt are ours and unvalidated; batch size and threshold rules were chosen once. (vii) We did not run the project's distilgpt2 or hosted-model configurations, its external-baseline detectors, or its sequential e-value component.

\section{Conclusion}
A $10^{-3}$ false-positive claim needs independent units behind it. On held-out honest batches the shipped p-values of the two most-used detectors flag nearly every honest batch in two scenarios, yet recalibration on about 20 independent batches restores a usable detector. With LLM agents, what is said and what is done can disagree, labels by instruction are fragile to wording, and text auditors that agree with the labels for open-weight agents can disagree with them for others. Positive controls therefore need a behavioural check, and honest baselines must come from the model family under audit.

\section{Code and Data}\label{sec:code}
\url{https://github.com/shoumyac/collusion-evidence-audit}. Pre-existing and unmodified: the starter repository \cite{starter} (commit \texttt{e6fb1308}). New: all scripts, raw per-batch outputs, LLM transcripts, a tested audit kit and this report.

\emph{Audit kit} (\texttt{kit/collusion\_audit\_kit.py}, no dependencies beyond the standard library). It provides: \texttt{count\_distinct\_runs} (raw, within-file and global distinct runs from appended JSONL transcripts); \texttt{batch\_threshold}, which refuses an $\alpha$ the number of independent honest batches cannot support (it needs at least $1/\alpha-1$); \texttt{heldout\_rates} (random half splits, FPR on the held-out half and TPR on colluding batches); \texttt{manipulation\_check} (price difference with bootstrap CI and position between Nash and the joint maximum, flagging a label the behaviour does not support); and \texttt{auroc\_with\_ci}. Six unit tests cover appended duplicate runs, Wilson intervals, the batch-count guard, held-out FPR near $\alpha$, a null manipulation effect and an inverted AUROC; run \texttt{cd kit \&\& python -m pytest -q}. Every number is produced by \texttt{sprint\_work/f\_report\_assets.py} and \texttt{g\_make\_tables.py} from saved files. No credentials are in the repository. Info-hazard note: we release no new covert-channel designs.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}
\paragraph{Limitations.} See Section~\ref{sec:disc}. In addition: all scripted-agent results depend on the starter project's agent designs; thresholds calibrated here do not transfer to other tasks or models; sample sizes are small where noted (4--5 batches per LLM condition, so batch-level flag counts are coarse); the hosted-model and distilgpt2 scenarios were not run.
\paragraph{Dual use.} Understanding how detectors and text auditors fail could help design colluding agents that evade them. We mitigate this by reporting failures of calibration and auditing at a general level, releasing no covert channel or evasion strategy beyond those already in the public starter repository, and proposing stricter evidence standards rather than evasion techniques. Our prompts ask LLM firms to coordinate prices in a toy simulated market; several models refused or only signalled tacitly, which we report. The prompts give no operational guidance for real markets.
\paragraph{Ethics.} No human subjects and no personal data; all data are synthetic or from public repositories. Compute and API use were funded by sprint credits and the authors; spend was monitored.

\section{Reproducibility details}
Starter commit \texttt{e6fb1308dae9cf97ae42aec1c5f7889d9e2291e8}; Python 3.11 (numpy 1.26.4). Held-out batch seeds: 100000+ (honest, keyword), 200000+ (sparse), 700000+ (smart), 800000+ (adaptive), extra honest shards 400000+, 500000+, 600000+; LLM seeds 300000+ plus a per-condition offset. Open-weight models served with vLLM 0.6.6 on Modal (temperature 0.7, max 200 reply tokens, context 4096); Claude models: reply cap 400 tokens (900 for Sonnet prompt 2), thinking disabled where the API allows, default sampling. The model IDs used were \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} and \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}; the API's own response returned the same strings in its \texttt{model} field, and the auditor summaries in the repository record the auditor ID. Earlier Claude runs with a 120-token cap were discarded because truncation inflated prices; they are archived and not used. Refusal regex: \texttt{(can't|cannot|won't|not going to|will not|unable to|decline)} within 80 characters before \texttt{(collu|coordinat|antitrust|illegal)}. Price-stability regex: \texttt{undercut|race to the bottom|match your price|keep prices|hold(ing)? (at|prices)|mutual}. The auditor prompt asks for a JSON score of 0--100 for evidence of coordination on higher prices than independent competition would produce.

\section{Leakage check for the message classifier}
The naive-Bayes classifier is cross-validated with whole runs as the unit (a run is never split between train and test folds). Table~\ref{tab:w} lists, for each agent model, the eight words most indicative of each condition after removing every word that appears in the prompts (words in at least 5 runs). They are content words that differ in what the firms say, not words copied from the instructions. For Claude Haiku they are refusal vocabulary (``price-fixing'', ``antitrust'', ``law'') in the collusion condition and price-war talk (``undercut'', ``capture'') in the honest condition, which agrees with the transcript examples in Appendix C.
